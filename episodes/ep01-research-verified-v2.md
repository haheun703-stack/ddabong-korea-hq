# DDABONG KOREA — EP01 Verified Research v2

Date: 2026-08-16
Episode: Gyeongju Royal Tombs

## Core thesis
The safest and strongest DDABONG angle is not “we know exactly what Silla people thought.” It is:

**The scale, structure, grave goods, and gold regalia show deliberate choices about rank and authority. Official museum interpretation explicitly connects Silla elite gold burial culture with sacredness, legitimacy, and enduring authority. We can use that evidence to reconstruct the problem their society may have been solving, while clearly labeling the mindset layer as interpretation.**

## Source map

### S1 — UNESCO World Heritage Centre, Gyeongju Historic Areas
Use for:
- Gyeongju as a major Silla heritage landscape.
- Tumuli Park Belt consists of three groups of royal tombs.
- Most mounds are domed.
- Tombs contain double wooden coffins covered with gravel.
- Excavations revealed rich grave goods including gold, glass, and fine ceramics.
- The five heritage belts together preserve evidence of Silla city layout, social structure, and ways of life.

Source: https://whc.unesco.org/en/list/976/

### S2 — Gyeongju National Museum, “Silla, Land of Gold”
Use for the DDABONG mindset backbone.
The museum states that during the height of Silla gold culture in the 5th to early 6th century, the ruling elite constructed huge tombs in the royal capital, adorned the tomb occupants with elaborate gold objects, and used gold to express **sacredness, legitimacy, and eternal authority**.

This is our strongest official support for connecting elite burial display with authority.

Source: https://gyeongju.museum.go.kr/kor/html/sub04/0403.html?mode=V&no=5053

### S3 — Gyeongju National Museum, Special Exhibition “Cheonma, Fly Again”
Use for Cheonmachong structure and discovery facts.
- Excavated in 1973 as Hwangnam-dong Tomb No. 155.
- It was a test excavation before the larger Hwangnamdaechong excavation.
- 11,526 artifacts were recovered.
- Named Cheonmachong in 1974 after the birch-bark saddle flap painted with the heavenly horse.
- Mound diameter: 47 m; height: 12.7 m.
- Wooden outer chamber: 6.6 m × 4.2 m.
- Coffin: 2.15 m × 0.8 m.
- Stones were piled above the wooden chamber and covered with earth.
- Gold crown, earrings, ornaments, weapons, vessels, lacquerware, glass, horse gear and other goods were placed with the deceased and in the grave-goods chest.

Source: https://gyeongju.museum.go.kr/kor/html/sub02/0202.html?d_mng_no=110&mode=VD

### S4 — National Research Institute of Cultural Heritage, Archaeology Dictionary — Cheonmachong
Use for occupant uncertainty and status.
- The scale of the tomb and quality of excavated objects indicate the deceased was probably a Silla king or a person of rank comparable to a king.
- Several chronological and identity hypotheses exist; the identity is not certain.

Source: https://portal.nrich.go.kr/kor/archeologyUsrView.do?idx=5195&menuIdx=797&sk=%EC%B2%9C%EB%A7%88%EC%B4%9D&st_char=

### S5 — NRICH, “Cheonmachong and East Asian Tomb Culture” (2023 conference volume)
Use as academic framing, not as a shortcut to conclusions we have not read in full.
The official conference volume includes papers titled:
- “Reconsidering the Structure of Above-ground Wooden-chamber Tombs with Stone Mounds through Cheonmachong”
- “Composition and Characteristics of Cheonmachong Grave Goods”
- **“The Process of Cheonmachong Funeral Rites and Strategies of Representation”**

This confirms that funeral process and representational strategy are legitimate scholarly research questions. We should not attribute a specific conclusion to that paper until the full paper is reviewed.

Source: https://portal.nrich.go.kr/kor/originalUsrView.do?info_idx=9001&menuIdx=1046

### S6 — Korea Heritage / government description of the Cheonmachong Heavenly Horse painting
Use for the object itself.
- Found in Tomb No. 155 in 1973.
- Painted on a saddle flap constructed from layers of birch bark.
- Dated to Silla in the 5th–6th century.

Reference: https://www.korea.kr/news/policyNewsView.do?newsId=148957403

## Claim safety table

### CONFIRMED / safe narration
- Gyeongju contains concentrated Silla royal-tomb groups. [S1]
- Cheonmachong was excavated in 1973 and was originally Tomb No. 155. [S3]
- Its structure used a wooden chamber, stone mound and earthen covering. [S3]
- Thousands of objects, including a gold crown and horse gear, were recovered. [S3]
- The exact identity of the deceased is uncertain. [S4]
- Silla elite gold burial culture is officially interpreted as expressing sacredness, legitimacy and enduring authority. [S2]

### INTERPRETATION / safe only with cautious language
- The funeral was not merely a private farewell; its scale suggests a large organized social event.
- Burial display may have communicated hierarchy and authority to the living.
- The death of a ruler may have created a need to reaffirm social and political order.
- The mound may have operated as a visible statement of rank and memory.

Use language such as:
- “may have”
- “allows us to ask whether…”
- “one way to understand this is…”
- “we cannot know their exact thoughts, but…”

### DO NOT STATE AS FACT
- “Silla people believed the king remained politically powerful after death.”
- “The tomb was built specifically to prevent political instability.”
- “A Silla official ordered the tomb to demonstrate continuity of government.”
- Any invented quotation presented as historical speech.

## Research conclusion for Script v2
The strongest narration pivot is now evidence-based:

**“We do not know exactly what a Silla official thought. But the National Museum itself describes this gold burial culture as a way the ruling elite expressed sacredness, legitimacy and enduring authority. That means the tomb was not only about hiding a body. It was also a carefully constructed display of who this person was — and what their status meant.”**

This should replace broader unsupported language about “the order of society” whenever possible.
